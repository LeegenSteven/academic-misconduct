Genetic correlation and causation of craniofacial microsomia with 33 diseases in Asian populations: insights from large-scale genome-wide cross-trait analysis

Craniofacial microsomia (CFM) is an uncommon birth defect that arises from the suboptimal development of the first and second branchial arches1. It often manifests as incomplete development of the unilateral craniofacial bones and surrounding soft tissues and nerves, leading to facial asymmetry. It may be accompanied by ipsilateral auricle deformity, auricle attachment deformity, giant mouth deformity, and facial skin hyperplasia1. The incidence rate of CFM is approximately 1 in 5,600 to 1 in 3,0002. CFM typically manifests on the right side of the face. However, it affects both sides in approximately 10% of cases. The condition can vary in intensity, potentially leading to difficulties in nutrient consumption, respiration, auditory function, and facial muscle operation2. Furthermore, CFM might be linked to deformities of the spine, heart, lungs, brain, and genitourinary tract3.

body/p ordinal=2; id=Par24

CFM is a congenital malformation caused by genetic factors. In sporadic cases of CFM, various chromosomal abnormalities and structural variations have been described4,5. These chromosomal abnormalities and genetic mutations may cause other diseases. For example, Berenguer et al. reported that 89% of oculo-auriculo-vertebral spectrum (OAVS) patients with mutations in MYT1 developed vertebral deformities, such as cervical depression and cervical fusion6. Timberlake et al. reported that mutations in the SF3B2 gene can also cause spinal deformity7. In addition, Zamariolli et al. identified a mutation at rs186536735 in the YPEL1 gene that may cause lung hypoplasia in a cohort of 73 Brazilian CFM patients8. Therefore, CFM may be genetically correlated with other diseases.

body/p ordinal=3; id=Par25

The linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) technique was devised to ascertain the existence of a genetic link between different diseases9. To date, few studies have methodically evaluated the genetic intersections, shared risk genes, and causal links between CFM and other diseases within Asian populations. The PLACO tool, which employs data from Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS), enables the exploration of a multitude of genetic alterations or genomic regions in relation to different characteristics10,11. Exploring the common genetic structure between CFM and other diseases is highly important, so the main purpose of our study was to explore the genetic associations, colocalized genes, and causal relationships between CFM and 33 diseases (including tumours and respiratory, heart and kidney diseases).

body/p ordinal=4; id=Par26

First, we analysed the genetic correlation between CFM and 33 diseases in an Asian population. We subsequently identified the polymorphic loci and genes involved in CFM and diseases genetically related to CFM via PLACO. In addition, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on these genes12,13. Finally, we used Two-sample Mendelian randomized analysis to analyse the causal relationship between CFM and tumours, respiratory disease, heart disease and kidney disease (Fig. 1).

GWAS summary data from a previously reported Chinese population with CFM are used here14. The cohort was composed of 1,056 males and 326 females with a mean age of 11.9 years old (s.d.: 6.5; range 4–48 years). To ensure high-quality data for GWAS research, the research team screened the dataset during the discovery phase on the basis of the following criteria: a sample call rate exceeding 99%; a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) call rate exceeding 95%; and a Hardy‒Weinberg equilibrium threshold set at 0.0001 in the control group (determined by Fisher’s exact test). Additionally, to avoid the inclusion of closely related individuals, they performed genome-wide identity by descent analysis for each pair of samples. Due to the limited detection capability of rare variants in association studies, they retained only SNPs with a minor allele frequency greater than 0.01 15. Bonferroni correction was applied to multivariable comparisons to reduce the risk of false-positive results, and the threshold for genome-wide significance was set at a P value less than 6.3 × 10^−8 (= 0.05/792,342 variants). Detailed information on sample characteristics, study design, and statistical analysis is described in a previous study14.

body/p ordinal=8; id=Par29

The GWAS summary data for 33 diseases including cancer, respiratory diseases and heart diseases were obtained from BioBank Japan (BBJ)15. The BBJ project had recruited approximately 200,000 participants in the first five years of the study. Among the participants, 53.1% were male. The average age of the male patients was 62.7 years old, and that of female patients was 61.5 years old15. The BBJ researchers carefully reviewed the distribution of all numerical data to rule out any outliers for each variable. The data concerning 33 diseases including cancer, respiratory diseases and heart diseases are shown in Table S1.

body/p ordinal=9; id=Par30

The method of genetic correlation analysis was described in our previous study16. Following the standard approach recommended by developers, LDSC software (https://github.com/bulik/ldsc) was used here to evaluate the genetic correlations between CFM and 33 diseases. LDSC can estimate genetic correlation via GWAS summary statistics rather than via individual-level genotype data17,18. The idea underlying LDSC is that, for a polygenic trait, linkage disequilibrium (LD) with an annotation that is enriched for heritability will increase the χ2 statistic of a SNP more than LD with an annotation that is not enriched for heritability17. In addition, SNPs with lower LD have significantly greater per-SNP heritability. Consequently, LDSC is capable of distinguishing genuine polygenicity from confounding bias (such as cryptic relatedness and population stratification) and is more powerful than λGC, especially for large sample sizes. If genetic correlations are statistically and quantitatively significant, then we can determine that total phenotypic correlations cannot be attributed fully to environmental confounders. After correcting for multiple testing such as Bonferroni correction, the significant threshold of this study should be P < 0.0015 (0.05/33 = 0.0015). When the P value in the LDSC analysis is less than 0.05, we consider that there may be a genetic correlation between the two diseases.

body/p ordinal=10; id=Par31

We adopted the research method of Yu et al.11. A pleiotropic analysis under the composite null hypothesis (PLACO) was used to identify pleiotropy among CFM and 33 diseases at the SNP level. SNPs that reached the genome-wide significance level (P < 5 × 10–8) were considered pleiotropic variants. Functional mapping and annotation (FUMA) of GWAS was used to determine the genomic regions of these risk variants (i.e., pleiotropic loci)19. Additionally, a Bayesian colocalization analysis was conducted to determine the pleiotropic loci shared by CFM and other diseases found to be genetically associated with LDSC20. To explore the shared mechanisms of the identified loci, nearby genes were mapped on the basis of lead SNPs within each locus. Additionally, a generalized gene set analysis of GWAS data (multimarker analysis of genomic annotation, MAGMA) was used to determine the biological function of these pleiotropic loci. Specifically, we performed MAGMA gene analysis to identify pleiotropic genes by properly incorporating LD between markers and to detect multimarker effects (P < 0.05/18,345 = 2.73 × 10–6)21. Gene sets were obtained from MsigDB v5.2. A total of 10,894 gene sets (curated gene sets: 4728, GO terms: 6166) were tested22. Genome-wide tissue-specific enrichment analysis was conducted on the basis of 54 GTEx tissues for the genome-wide pleiotropic results generated by PLACO23.

body/p ordinal=11; id=Par32

In this study, we employed CFM as the exposure factor and 33 diseases including cancer, respiratory diseases and heart diseases as outcome factors to conduct two-sample MR analysis. The following three suppositions were made using MR analysis: first, SNPs are significantly associated with the exposure; second, SNPs are not related to confounding factors; and last, SNPs do not have a direct relationship with the outcome but are only influenced indirectly through the exposure.

body/p ordinal=12; id=Par33

Initially, we selected SNPs with a genome-wide significant association with exposure (p < 5 × 10–6) to serve as instrumental variables during further analyses, aiming to increase specificity. We then confirmed that none of the selected SNPs had strong linkage disequilibrium (LD), as this could bias the findings. SNPs with a minor allele frequency less than 0.01 were excluded. We then manually checked and eliminated all identified SNPs associated with confounders. We used the PhenoScanner GWAS database to rule out confounding factors associated with the outcome.

We used five MR methods, namely, IVW, weighted median28, simple mode, weighted mode29,30, and MR‒Egger31,32, to verify the causal relationships between CFM and these 36 diseases. The IVW model was employed for principal analysis, as it is renowned for its reliability in delivering inferential estimates of causality, especially in scenarios devoid of pleiotropy indications33,34.

body/p ordinal=15; id=Par36

We used an intercept of MR‒Egger regression to detect horizontal pleiotropy35. We used Cochran’s Q statistic to examine the heterogeneity of IV in the analysis. A p value less than 0.05 indicated significant heterogeneity. To analyse the potential effect of the SNPs, a leave-one-out test was conducted.

body/p ordinal=16; id=Par37

The beta value and odds ratio (OR) were used to represent causal effects between exposure and outcome variables. In the main analysis, p values for the exposure (p < 0.05) were used to denote significance.

body/p ordinal=17; id=Par38

R software version 4.2.2 was used for principal statistical examination. The LDSC method was applied using the “LDSC” tool, version 1.0.1. PLACO analysis was conducted via the “PLACO” package. Bayesian colocalization was handled with the “coloc” package, version 5.2.1. Functional analysis was carried out via the FUMA online utility. Gene-centric and gene-set analyses were executed with MAGMA, whereas two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis was facilitated by the “TwoSampleMR” package, version 0.5.7.

We assessed the genetic associations between CFM and 33 diseases in an Asian population. Through LDSC analysis, we found that thyroid cancer (rg = −4.298, p = 0.001) has a negative genetic correlation with CFM. Additionally, we also revealed that two other diseases may have genetic associations with CFM, namely malignant lymphoma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Among them, malignant lymphoma (rg = −1.594, p = 0.026) may have a negative genetic correlation with CFM, while chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (rg = 0.739, p = 0.018) may have a positive genetic correlation with CFM (Table 1).

Given that LDSC identified genetic correlations between CFM and malignant lymphoma, thyroid cancer, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, we used PLACO to identify potential multiple effective loci for these diseases (Supplementary File 1: Figure S1). Through screening and colocalization analysis, a total of 172 potential multi-effect loci (P < 5 × 10−8) were ultimately identified, of which 58 were shared loci between CFM and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), 62 were shared loci between CFM and malignant lymphoma, and 52 were shared loci between CFM and thyroid cancer (Figure S1, Table S2). Among these loci, there were a total of 9 loci with a PP.H4 greater than 0.7, of which 7 were shared loci between CFM and COPD, 1 was shared between CFM and malignant lymphoma, and 1 was shared between CFM and thyroid cancer (Table 2). By annotating these 9 SNPs via FUMA, a total of 4 potential common effect genes were identified (DCDC2, SH2B3, and ATXN2 may be common effect genes for COPD and CFM, whereas UBE3D is a common effect gene for malignant lymphoma and CFM) (Table 2). The regional maps of each trait pair are presented in Supplementary File 1: Figures S2 to S4. MAGMA analysis of the colocalized SNPs identified by PLACO identified a total of 1760 shared genes (P < 0.05/18345 = 2.726e-6), of which 612 were shared between CFM and COPD, 588 were shared between CFM and malignant lymphoma, and 560 were shared between CFM and thyroid cancer (Fig. 2, Table S3). Further tissue-specific analysis revealed that these risk loci were enriched in several tissues, such as the blood, liver, and spleen (Table S4, Figure S5).

We performed LDSC analysis between 33 diseases and CFM and report that malignant lymphoma, thyroid cancer and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease are genetically related to CFM. Furthermore, we used PLACO to identify loci and genes shared between these diseases and CFM. We subsequently conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on these genes and found that these three diseases share common signalling pathways with CFM. Finally, we conducted MR analysis and found that CFM can reduce the incidence of urolithiasis. We provide strong evidence that these three diseases share a common genetic mechanism with CFM. A previous study reported that a case of malignant lymphoma occurred in the OAVS family36. However, there is currently no comprehensive clinical or basic research on the relationships among thyroid cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and CFM. Our study provides a new perspective for the study of CFM and these three diseases.

body/p ordinal=40; id=Par53

We found a series of shared genetic risk loci related to CFM and COPD, malignant lymphoma and thyroid cancer. Through KEGG enrichment analysis of these genes, we found that the calcium signalling pathway is a common enrichment pathway for these shared genes, which suggests that the calcium signalling pathway may be a common mechanism between CFM and COPD, malignant lymphoma and thyroid cancer. Kapil et al. reported that CaV1.2 is active in the primary and secondary pharyngeal arches and is localized to the specific cell subset responsible for the development of the jaw’s foundational structures37. In addition, they reported that Ca2+ regulates the hypertrophy and proliferation of mandibular chondrocytes through CaV1.2 influx and that changes in CaV1.2 activity affects the development of the mouse mandible37. Shin et al. reported that diminishing the TRPC1/TRPC3-mediated calcium signalling pathway offers protection against oxidative stress-induced COPD38. In addition, the function of bronchial epithelial cells is regulated by Ca2+ signals produced by ion channels, receptors and intracellular organelles39. Bronchial epithelial cells play pivotal roles as effectors in the development of COPD40. Fedor et al. reviewed the role of the calcium signalling pathway in B-cell lymphomas41. Studies have shown that a large proportion of mutations in B-cell lymphoma occur in genes with calcium-dependent signalling functions42,43. Shawna and colleagues reported that various lymphomas exhibit distinct expression profiles of calcium channels and their associated signalling mechanisms. These findings suggest that L-type calcium channels and calcium-dependent pathways might be utilized as distinctive markers for personalized lymphoma therapies44. Soazig et al. reported that calcium ions are involved in the meticulous control of mitochondrial formation in follicular thyroid carcinoma cells45. Our MR analysis revealed that CFM can reduce the incidence of urolithiasis, which may also be related to the calcium signalling pathway. Sarah et al. reported that genetic variants that affect calcium-sensitive receptor signalling are associated with serum calcium concentrations, urinary calcium excretion, and many kidney stone episodes46. In addition, Eugenia et al. reported that pH-induced Ca2+ changes promote calcium kidney stones47.

Our research has several advantages. First, the extensive genetic overlap between CFM and other diseases can help in discovering potential genetic links that cannot be observed in a single phenotypic study. Second, advances in understanding the common genetic structure of CFM are critical for developing new genetics-based treatment strategies. Targeted therapies designed for one disease are likely to have broader therapeutic effects on other diseases, which means a wider range of patients may benefit from such research. Third, the negative causal relationship between CFM and urolithiasis provides important insights into the development of clinical prevention and treatment strategies.

body/p ordinal=42; id=Par55

Our research also has several potential limitations. First, although our results suggest that the comorbidity of CFM with other diseases can be explained in part by common genetic components, the common biological mechanisms are largely unclear. Second, the functional role of these pleiotropic genes is still completely unknown, so further experimental and methodological research is necessary. Exploring the pleiotropic nature of the rare genetic loci behind CFM will undoubtedly provide a deeper understanding of the common genetic basis between these diseases. Third, our research subjects were mainly Asian populations, and the results may not be applicable to other races (such as European populations). Therefore, future research needs to explore the genetic correlations and causal relationships in different populations to ensure universality. Fourth, there is a large imbalance in the sample sizes of these diseases, resulting in different test powers. Finally, our research has identified pleiotropic loci and genes that may have common effects in CFM and other diseases. However, the functional roles of these genes in CFM and other diseases remain unclear. Therefore, further animal models, cell culture experiments, or other molecular biology techniques are needed to understand the underlying mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets.

body/p ordinal=43; id=Par56

Our study reveals for the first time in an Asian population the genetic associations of CFM with three diseases (malignant lymphoma, thyroid cancer and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), which share pleiotropic risk loci and genes. In addition, we found that there is a common calcium signalling pathway between these diseases and CFM. Finally, our results reveal a causal relationship between CFM and urolithiasis.
